Structural Heart Disease

Innovative Care for Structural Heart Conditions

What is Structural Heart Disease?

Structural Heart Disease refers to abnormalities in the heart's valves, walls, or chambers, often affecting its ability to pump blood efficiently. These conditions may be congenital (present at birth) or acquired over time due to aging, infections, or other heart conditions. Advances in medical technology have introduced minimally invasive treatments, offering better outcomes for patients.

Key Features of Structural Heart Disease

  • Includes conditions like heart valve disease, septal defects, and cardiomyopathies
  • May cause symptoms like shortness of breath, fatigue, or chest pain
  • Requires precise diagnosis using advanced imaging techniques
  • Treatable through both minimally invasive procedures and traditional surgery

Symptoms of Structural Heart Disease

Symptoms vary based on the specific condition but may include:

  • Shortness of breath during activity or at rest
  • Fatigue or reduced exercise capacity
  • Chest pain or discomfort
  • Swelling in the legs, ankles, or feet
  • Heart palpitations or irregular heartbeats
  • Dizziness, fainting, or near-fainting episodes

Causes and Risk Factors

Structural heart disease can arise from various causes and risk factors, including:

  • Congenital Heart Defects: Abnormalities present from birth
  • Aging: Degeneration of heart valves over time
  • Infections: Conditions like endocarditis damaging heart structures
  • Heart Attacks: Causing damage to heart walls or valves
  • Hypertension: Long-term high blood pressure can strain the heart
  • Genetics: Family history of structural heart defects

How is Structural Heart Disease Diagnosed and Treated?

Diagnosis involves comprehensive imaging and functional tests:

  • Echocardiography: Ultrasound imaging to assess heart structure and function
  • Cardiac MRI or CT: Provides detailed images of the heart
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG): Detects electrical abnormalities
  • Cardiac Catheterization: Measures pressures and blood flow within the heart

Treatment options depend on the condition's severity and location:

  • Medications: Manage symptoms and prevent complications
  • Minimally Invasive Procedures: Includes transcatheter valve replacement (TAVR) and septal defect closures
  • Open-Heart Surgery: Repairs or replaces damaged valves or other structures
  • Watchful Waiting: Monitoring for mild or asymptomatic conditions

Risks and Potential Complications

Untreated structural heart disease can lead to serious complications, such as:

  • Heart failure due to impaired blood flow
  • Stroke from blood clots forming in the heart
  • Arrhythmias or irregular heart rhythms
  • Infections such as endocarditis
  • Sudden cardiac arrest in severe cases

Recovery and Long-Term Management

Recovery from structural heart disease treatment depends on the procedure performed. Common steps include:

  • Gradual return to physical activity as recommended by a healthcare provider
  • Adherence to prescribed medications to manage symptoms and prevent complications
  • Regular follow-ups with a cardiologist to monitor heart function
  • Participation in cardiac rehabilitation programs for strength and recovery
  • Maintaining a heart-healthy lifestyle with proper diet and exercise

With timely intervention and ongoing care, patients with structural heart disease can enjoy improved quality of life and heart health.
